TAAI 2026 Keynote Speech I · Day 1, Fri, Nov 20, 2026 · 09:30–10:20

Beyond Words: Riemannian Geometry for Next-Generation Graph Foundation Models

Prof. Philip S. Yu

UIC Distinguished Professor and Wexler Chair in Information Technology, Department of Computer Science, University of Illinois Chicago · ACM / IEEE / AAAS Fellow

Abstract

Graph Foundation Models (GFMs) are poised to transform graph learning, yet substantial debate remains over how to construct a general-purpose GFM analogous to Large Language Models (LLMs). Traditional Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) struggle with memory retention, principled interpretability, and multi-domain adaptation, while graph serialization limits the direct application of LLMs, as linear tokens fail to capture the rich structural complexity of graphs. In contrast, Riemannian geometry provides a natural, mathematically principled framework for modeling graph structures, offering compatibility with semantic graph learning and LLM integration. In this talk, we argue that for graphs, geometry speaks louder than words. We introduce Riemannian Graph Foundations, which prioritize intrinsic graph geometry and endow models with endogenous capabilities for structural inference and generation, moving beyond simple representation-space transformations. Recent results on building a graph foundation model within Riemannian geometric space will be presented, highlighting how this approach lays the groundwork for next-generation graph intelligence.

Biography

Dr. Philip S. Yu is a Fellow of the ACM, IEEE, and AAAS. Dr. Yu received the ACM SIGKDD 2016 Innovation Award for his influential research and scientific contributions to mining, fusion, and anonymization of big data; the IEEE Computer Society 2013 Technical Achievement Award for “pioneering and fundamentally innovative contributions to the scalable indexing, querying, searching, mining, and anonymization of big data”; and the Research Contributions Award from ICDM in 2003 for his pioneering work in data mining. He has published over 2,000 refereed conference and journal papers, which have been cited more than 278,000 times, with an H-index of 223. He has also applied for more than 300 patents. Dr. Yu served as Editor-in-Chief of ACM TKDD (2011–2017) and IEEE TKDE (2001–2004).

TAAI 2026 Keynote Speech II · Day 1, Fri, Nov 20, 2026 · 16:00–16:50

Neural Speech Enhancement and Assessment and Their Applications in Assistive Oral Communication Technologies

Prof. Yu Tsao

Research Fellow, Academia Sinica, Taiwan

Abstract

This presentation is divided into three parts. Firstly, we will discuss our recent advancements in neural speech enhancement (SE), a critical element in various speech-related applications. The primary objective of SE is to enhance speech signals by mitigating distortions caused by additive and convoluted noises, thereby improving human-human and human-machine communication efficacy. We'll delve into the system architecture and fundamental theories behind neural SE approaches, as well as explore important directions aimed at achieving better performance. Moving on to the second part, we will focus on our recent progress in neural speech assessment (SA), which aims to effectively evaluate the quality and intelligibility of spoken audio—a crucial aspect in numerous speech-related applications. Traditionally, the evaluation process often relies on listening tests involving human participants, which can be both resource-intensive and impractical due to the need for a large number of listeners. To address this challenge, neural SA metrics have garnered notable attention. We will discuss the fundamental systems of neural SA, highlight several factors influencing performance, and explore emerging trends in this domain. Finally, we will present some applications of neural SE and SA in assistive oral communication technologies. These applications include impaired speech transformation and noise reduction for assistive hearing and speaking devices. Through these discussions, our aim is to illustrate the potential impact of neural-based approaches in improving communication accessibility for individuals with oral communication disorders.

Biography

Yu Tsao (Senior Member, IEEE) received the B.S. and M.S. degrees in Electrical Engineering from National Taiwan University, Taipei, Taiwan, in 1999 and 2001, respectively, and the Ph.D. degree in Electrical and Computer Engineering from the Georgia Institute of Technology, Atlanta, GA, USA, in 2008. From 2009 to 2011, he was a Researcher at the National Institute of Information and Communications Technology (NICT), Tokyo, Japan, where he conducted research and product development in multilingual speech-to-speech translation systems, focusing on automatic speech recognition. He is currently a Research Fellow (Professor) and the Deputy Director at the Research Center for Information Technology Innovation, Academia Sinica, Taipei, Taiwan. He also holds a joint appointment as a Professor in the Department of Electrical Engineering at Chung Yuan Christian University, Taoyuan, Taiwan. His research interests include assistive oral communication technologies, audio coding, and bio-signal processing. He serves as an Associate Editor for IEEE Transactions on Consumer Electronics and IEEE Signal Processing Letters. He received the Outstanding Research Award from Taiwan’s National Science and Technology Council (NSTC), the 2025 IEEE Chester W. Sall Memorial Award, and served as the corresponding author of a paper that won the 2021 IEEE Signal Processing Society Young Author Best Paper Award.

TAAI 2026 Keynote Speech III · Day 2, Sat, Nov 21, 2026 · 09:00–09:50

AI-Driven Network Traffic Analysis for Anomaly Detection and Cybersecurity

Prof. Ljiljana Trajkovic

Professor, Simon Fraser University, Canada · IEEE Fellow

Abstract

Collection and analysis of data from deployed networks is essential for understanding communication networks. Hence, data mining and statistical analysis of network data have been employed to determine traffic loads, analyze patterns of users' behavior, predict future network traffic, and detect traffic anomalies. The Internet has historically been prone to failures and attacks that significantly degrade its performance, affect the Internet connectivity, and cause routing disconnections. Frequent cases of various cyber threats have been encountered over the years and, hence, detection of anomalous behavior is a topic of great interest in cybersecurity. In described case studies, traffic traces collected by various collection sites are used to classify network anomalies. Various anomaly and intrusion detection approaches based on machine learning have been employed to analyze collected data. Deep learning, broad learning, gradient boosted decision trees, and reservoir computing algorithms were used to develop models based on collected datasets that contain Internet worms, viruses, power outages, ransomware events, router misconfigurations, Internet Protocol hijacks, and infrastructure failures in times of conflict. The reported results indicate that while performance of machine learning models greatly depends on the used datasets, they are viable tools for detecting the Internet anomalies.

Biography

Ljiljana Trajkovic received the Dipl. Ing. degree from University of Pristina, Yugoslavia, the M.Sc. degrees in electrical engineering and computer engineering from Syracuse University, Syracuse, NY, and the Ph.D. degree in electrical engineering from University of California at Los Angeles. She is currently a professor in the School of Engineering Science, Simon Fraser University, Burnaby, British Columbia, Canada. Her research interests include communication networks and dynamical systems. Dr. Trajkovic served as IEEE Division X Delegate/Director, President of the IEEE Systems, Man, and Cybernetics Society, and President of the IEEE Circuits and Systems Society. She serves as Editor-in-Chief of the IEEE Transactions on Human-Machine Systems. She is a Distinguished Lecturer of the IEEE Systems, Man, and Cybernetics Society and was a Distinguished Lecturer of the IEEE Circuits and System Society. She is a Fellow of the IEEE.

TAAI 2026 Keynote Speech IV · Day 2, Sat, Nov 21, 2026 · 15:40–16:30

Getting to Know You: Long- and Short-Term Adaptation in Dialogue Systems

Prof. Kazunori Komatani

Professor, SANKEN, The University of Osaka, Japan

Abstract

Large language models have made dialogue systems capable of generating fluent responses. However, being good with words is not the same as understanding the person one is interacting with. A dialogue agent should adapt not just to any user, but to the particular person it is talking to, whose knowledge, experiences, and feelings differ from those of others. In this talk, I will discuss our research on user-adaptive spoken dialogue systems from two complementary perspectives. The first is acquiring knowledge through interaction, particularly learning unfamiliar words by deciding when, what, and how to ask users. The second is understanding users' current states through multimodal sentiment estimation, using information from speech, facial expressions, and physiological signals. I will also discuss how these research topics relate to recent developments in agentic AI, particularly from the perspectives of state representation and action selection.

Biography

Kazunori Komatani is a Professor at SANKEN, the University of Osaka, Japan. He received his Doctor of Informatics degree from Kyoto University in 2002. He was an Assistant Professor at Kyoto University from 2002 to 2010 and an Associate Professor at Nagoya University from 2010 to 2014. He was also a Visiting Scientist at Carnegie Mellon University from 2008 to 2009. He joined SANKEN as a Professor in 2014. His research interests include spoken dialogue systems, dialogue management, user adaptation, and multimodal sentiment analysis.

Poster Presentation Guidelines

Poster size and orientation

  • Recommended size: A0 portrait (84.1 cm wide × 118.9 cm high).
  • Posters must not exceed A0 dimensions. Smaller formats, such as A1, are acceptable.
  • Portrait orientation is required. Landscape posters are not permitted.

Poster content

Posters should clearly include the paper title, authors, affiliations, motivation and objectives, methods, results, and conclusions.

Authors are encouraged to use clear figures, concise text, and fonts that are easily readable from approximately 1–2 meters away.

  • Presenters are responsible for printing and bringing their posters to the conference venue.
  • Each accepted poster paper will be assigned a display space. Poster boards and mounting materials will be provided.

Q1. Is the program final?

Not yet. This is the tentative program: session assignments, session names, session chairs, and times may still change. Please check this page for the latest version.

Q2. How do I find my paper?

Go to Paper Sessions and search by paper ID (for example 148), title, author, or keyword. Use Search in to search only one field, and the Topic, Day, and session-type filters to narrow the list. Each paper has its own link (for example program.html#paper-148) that you can share with co-authors.

Q3. Where do I find my paper ID?

Your paper ID is your OpenReview submission number, shown on your paper in OpenReview (external site, opens in a new tab) and next to each paper under Paper Sessions (for example, Paper #148). Please include it in every email about your paper.

Q4. Something about my paper is wrong, or I cannot find it. What should I do?

Email taai2026org@gmail.com with your paper ID by November 9, 2026. Please check your paper title, author names, session, and presentation time (poster papers: also your board number).

Q5. Can I change my session or presentation time? I have a scheduling conflict.

Sessions were arranged so that no author has two papers presented at the same time, and the presentation type (oral or poster) is assigned by the Organizing Committee. If you still have a conflict, email taai2026org@gmail.com with your paper IDs and the reason by November 9, 2026.

Q6. Which time zone are the times in?

All times on this page are Asia/Taipei time (UTC+8).

Q7. I have more than one accepted paper. How does that work in the program?

Each paper has its own slot; search Paper Sessions with Search in: Author to see all of yours. Each accepted paper also needs its own registration, with at least one author presenting it on-site. See Registration FAQ Q4.

Q8. What is the Program & Abstract Book?

It compiles the conference program with paper titles, authors, affiliations, abstracts, and session information. It is a record of the conference presentations, not the formal proceedings. See Call for Papers FAQ Q33.

Q9. Will there be a printed or online program book?

There is no printed program book: TAAI 2026 is fully digital. The Program & Abstract Book will be available as a PDF on this website before the conference. This page always has the latest program, including any changes made after the book is prepared.

Q10. Why don’t I see the High School, Special Session, or Industrial Track papers in Paper Sessions?

Paper Sessions lists the International and Domestic Track oral and poster papers. The other sessions in the timetable, such as the High School Oral Sessions (Day 2, Room C), Special Sessions, Industrial Tracks, and the TAAI Thesis Award presentations, are organized separately; see the timetable for their times and rooms.

Q11. How long is an oral presentation?

About 10 minutes per paper, including Q&A. Papers are presented in the order listed in each session, and each paper shows its start time under Paper Sessions.

Q12. What should I do before my oral session?

Check your session, room, and start time under Paper Sessions, and please be in the room before the session begins. Papers are presented in the order listed, so stay for the whole session if you can. Each session’s chair will be listed in the session once confirmed.

Q13. What equipment is available for oral presentations? How do I load my slides?

Each session room has a projector and a presentation laptop (Windows, with PowerPoint and a PDF reader), connected by HDMI. Please prepare 16:9 slides as PDF or PPTX and copy them to the room laptop from a USB drive during the break before your session; a PDF copy is a good backup. If you use your own laptop, bring an adapter for HDMI (for example, USB-C to HDMI) and test it before the session starts.

Q14. Which language should I present in?

International sessions are presented in English. Domestic sessions may be presented in Chinese or English; English slides are encouraged so that international attendees can follow.

Q15. How was my paper assigned to an oral or poster session?

The Organizing Committee assigns the presentation type based on review outcomes and program scheduling. Oral and poster are different paper types, not different review standards: International Track oral papers are published as Full Papers (12–15 pages) and poster papers as Short Papers (6–11 pages), in the same Springer CCIS post-proceedings. Domestic Extended Abstracts are, in principle, presented as posters. See Call for Papers FAQ Q19 and Q29.

Q16. What do I need to know for a poster presentation?

Each poster paper has a board number: IP- for international and DP- for domestic papers, shown next to the paper under Paper Sessions. Posters should be A0 portrait (A1 is also acceptable; landscape is not). Please print and bring your own poster; boards and mounting materials are provided. Set up before your session and stay to answer questions during it. Setup and removal times will be announced later. See the Poster Presentation Guidelines.

Q17. When do I put up and take down my poster?

Each poster session has its own boards, labeled with board numbers (IP-/DP-). Put up your poster at your board during the break before your session and take it down right after the session ends, so the board is ready for the next session. Mounting materials are provided; the board size and any setup details will be posted at the poster area.

Q18. I chose Presentation Only (NOT-to-Publish). Is my paper still in the program?

Yes. Presentation Only means the paper is not published in the proceedings; it does not change your oral or poster assignment or cancel your presentation slot. At least one author must still register and present on-site, and the title, authors, and abstract may still appear in program materials. See Camera-Ready FAQ Q7 and Call for Papers FAQ Q12.

Q19. Can a student or another co-author present my paper?

Yes. The presenting-author registration must be held by the author who will present the paper on-site, so the co-author who presents registers in their own name; you may pay the fee for them. See Registration FAQ Q19.

Q20. Can I present remotely?

Only with prior approval. On-site presentation is the default; authors who cannot travel for a justified reason may apply by November 10, 2026, 23:59 AoE. See Registration FAQ Q24 for how to apply and the video requirements.

Q21. What happens if no author presents the paper?

Every accepted paper needs at least one registered author who presents it on-site (or remotely with prior approval). A paper that is not presented may be removed from the program and cannot be published. If no author can attend, email taai2026org@gmail.com as early as possible. See Camera-Ready FAQ Q24 and Registration FAQ Q24.

Q22. Who chairs each session?

Session chairs are still being confirmed. They will be listed in each session under Paper Sessions (currently shown as TBA).

Q23. I am a session chair. What should I do?

Please arrive at your room about 10 minutes before the session, check that the presenters are there and their slides are loaded, introduce each speaker, keep each paper to its slot (about 8 minutes for the talk and 2 minutes for Q&A), moderate questions, and report any paper that is not presented to the registration desk after the session. Presenters: please introduce yourself to your session chair before the session starts.

Q24. When and where are the keynote speeches?

All keynotes are in the Plenary Hall (5F Lishan Hall): Keynote I on Day 1 at 09:30 (speaker to be announced), Keynote II on Day 1 at 16:00, Keynote III on Day 2 at 09:00, and Keynote IV on Day 2 at 15:40. The International Panel with the keynote speakers follows on Day 1 at 16:50. See Keynote Speeches.

Q25. Can I attend sessions other than my own?

Yes. Every registration type includes both conference days, keynotes, and all sessions, so you are welcome to attend any session in the program.

Q26. Does a presenting author need to register?

Yes. Every accepted paper needs at least one presenting author who has completed registration and payment by the camera-ready deadline, October 31, 2026, 23:59 AoE (High School Session: November 8, 2026, 23:59 AoE). Otherwise the paper may be removed from the program. See the Registration FAQ.

Q27. What does registration include? Is the banquet included?

All registration types include both conference days, keynotes and sessions, coffee/tea breaks, and lunches. Full Registrants and Presenting Authors also receive the conference kit and admission to the Opening & Closing Ceremony and the Conference Banquet; Student and Accompanying Person registrations can add a banquet ticket (NT$ 2,000). Q28. When is the registration desk open? Can I register on-site?
